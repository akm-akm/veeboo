Most automation pitches skip the only question that matters to an owner: when do I get my money back? You can answer it yourself in about five minutes, before you speak to anyone who sells automations. That includes me.

You need four numbers from your side and two from the builder's quote.

## The four numbers from your side

**1. Hours per week.** Pick one task and count the hours your team spends on it in a normal week. Do not guess. Ask the person who does it to keep a tally for five working days.
**2. Hourly cost.** Take what an hour of that person's time costs you: pay plus the taxes and benefits on top. This is not the rate you bill clients. If the freed hour would not be sold to anyone, use the cost.

**3. Share automated.** No automation takes all of a task. Some cases are odd and a person still has to check the results. For a well-defined task, somewhere between half and four fifths is a sensible range to plan with. If someone promises you the full amount, ask them what happens to the exceptions.

**4. Weeks per month.** Use 4.33. It keeps the sum honest across a year.

## The two numbers from the quote

- **Setup fee.** The one-off cost of building it.
- **Monthly cost.** The retainer, plus the software and usage bills you will pay directly. Ask for those to be estimated in writing, because they are easy to leave out.

## The sum

```
monthly value   = hours per week x 4.33 x hourly cost x share automated
monthly net     = monthly value - monthly cost
payback months  = setup fee / monthly net
```

If the monthly net is zero or negative, stop. The automation never pays back, however good the demo was.

## A worked example

These figures are made up to show the method. They are not results from a real client.

Picture an office where two people share one task: copying new enquiries from the website and the inbox into the CRM, then sending the first reply. Together they spend 10 hours a week on it. An hour of their time costs the business $30. The builder quotes a $3,000 setup fee and $600 a month, and you both agree the automation should handle about 70% of the work.

```
monthly value   = 10 x 4.33 x $30 x 0.70 = $909
monthly net     = $909 - $600           = $309
payback months  = $3,000 / $309         = 9.7
```

Nearly ten months. That is not a clear yes.

Now run the same quote for a busier office, where the task takes 20 hours a week.

```
monthly value   = 20 x 4.33 x $30 x 0.70 = $1,819
monthly net     = $1,819 - $600         = $1,219
payback months  = $3,000 / $1,219       = 2.5
```

Same automation, same price, and it pays for itself inside a quarter. Volume decides the answer far more than how clever the technology is.

## What counts as a good answer

This is my own rule of thumb, not an industry standard:

- **Under 6 months:** a comfortable yes.
- **6 to 12 months:** only if the task is stable and you expect volume to grow.
- **Over 12 months:** no, or make the scope smaller until the number improves.

## Value that is not hours

Sometimes the hours are small but the task still costs you money. A missed call that becomes a lost booking is the usual example. You can put that in the sum too, but be strict about it:

1. Count the misses for two weeks. Use your phone system's log, not memory.
2. Estimate how many of those would have become paying work. Be pessimistic.
3. Multiply by what one job is worth to you after costs, not the invoice total.

Add that to the monthly value. If the case only works when you assume every missed call was a sale, the case does not work.

## When not to automate

The arithmetic says no more often than people expect. These are the common reasons.

- **The task is rare.** An hour a week at $30 is about $130 a month. No setup fee survives that.
- **The process is not settled.** If the steps change every month, you will pay for the automation to be rebuilt every month. Write the process down and run it by hand until it stops changing.
- **A cheaper fix exists.** A saved reply, a form with required fields or a shared calendar sometimes removes most of the work for nothing. Try that first.
- **A mistake is very expensive.** Where one error costs more than a year of savings, keep a person in the loop and count their checking time in the sum.
- **Nobody will own it.** Someone on your team has to read the alerts and handle the exceptions. If nobody has ten minutes a day for that, the automation will quietly rot.

## Keep the numbers after you buy

If you go ahead, write the four numbers down and agree them with the builder before work starts. After the first full month, measure again. You are checking two things: did the hours really fall, and did the share automated match what was promised?

> A builder who is confident in the work will be glad to be measured against the sum. One who avoids it is telling you something.
